# The degenerate basis subalgebras of the Cayley–Dickson tower

## The claim

Every basis subalgebra of every Cayley–Dickson algebra A_n = CDⁿ(R) is one of a **finite,
stable** list of algebras at each dimension, and the list is generated by a single operation —
the **relative mirror** — applied to the tower nodes:

> **R_A(K) := K + K(e_c e) ⊂ CD(A)**, for K a basis hyperplane of A and e_c ∉ K.

When K is the founding copy of a double, R_A(K) is the mirror double M(K) of the paper
(Theorem 3.3). When K is any *other* hyperplane, R_A(K) is a "mirror with respect to the
wrong subalgebra", and these are exactly the degenerate subalgebras: Cawagas's [2+13] and
[0+15] hyperplanes of T, and their analogues at every dimension.

Three theorems organise the whole landscape, all proved in
`papers/mirror-sedenions/RELATIVE-ERASURE-PROOF.md` and machine-verified:

1. **Relative erasure.** CD(R_A(K)) ≅ CD(A) for *every* anticommutative sign-monomial A and
   *every* basis hyperplane K. The erasure theorem of the tower note is the founding-copy case.
   The proof uses only anticommutativity between K and its complementary coset — no
   associativity, no quaternion property, not even that A is a double.
2. **Absorption.** CD(B) ≅ A_k for every 2^{k−1}-dimensional basis subalgebra B of any A_n.
   One standard doubling erases *all* degeneracy, not just the mirror bit.
3. **Stability and generation.** For n ≥ k+1 the 2^k-dimensional basis subalgebras of A_n
   realise exactly the classes of the hyperplanes of A_{k+1}, and those are
   {A_k} ∪ {R_{A_k}(K) : K a hyperplane of A_k}. The relative mirror is the **only** source of
   new isomorphism types; the standard doubling contributes none.

So the answer to "what is the structure of the messed-up subalgebras" is: **they are all
mirrors — but mirrors taken with respect to a non-founding hyperplane.** And the answer to
"what does the structure teach" is that the mirror construction is not one exotic sibling of
CD but the generator of the entire subalgebra landscape of CD, with CD itself acting as the
eraser.

## The landscape

Census by graded isomorphism of every subgroup-spanned subalgebra (`check21`, registry in
`code/landscape_registry.pkl`). Graded isomorphism was decided by the **associator pattern**
criterion below, which reaches dimension 32 and 64 where the paper's GL(4,2) enumeration stops.

| A_7 | 128 | — | — | S 2046, S′ 651, X16.2 4557, X16.3 4557 | <!-- PENDING: check21 n=7 k=5 --> |

**Number of classes: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 at dimensions 4, 8, 16, 32, 64**, and the list at each
dimension is complete one level up and never grows again (Theorem 3; checked through A_7 at
dimension 16 over 11 811 subgroups). The 64-dimensional count comes from Theorem 3 directly
(`check25`): the 63 hyperplanes of A_6 fall into **15 orbits** (7 singletons, 8 orbits of
seven) and their relative mirrors are **15 pairwise non-isomorphic** algebras (distinct
already on the fingerprint (non-associative triples, zero-divisor count), confirmed by the
associator search), so |L_5| = 1 + 15 = 16. The three singletons f = 32, 48, 56 are the mirror
tower M(T), M²(S), M³(O) with zero-divisor counts 3160, 3280, 3392 — the b = 1, 2, 3 column
of the tower table in `mirror-tower.md`; the other twelve are relative mirrors with respect
to non-founding copies (four more singletons, eight orbits of seven), with counts from 3220
to 3584.

The counts are not free either. Writing G(n,k) for the Gaussian binomial (number of k-dim
subspaces of F₂ⁿ), the 16-dimensional census satisfies, for every n ≥ 5 computed,

    #S′ = G(n−1, 4),    #X16.2 = #X16.3 = 7·G(n−1, 4),    #S = G(n, 4) − 15·G(n−1, 4),

and this follows from the relative-mirror table below by an induction identical to the proof
of Theorem 3: every 16-dimensional class has exactly *one* hyperplane whose relative mirror is
S′ (except S′ itself, which has none) and exactly *one* whose relative mirror is S (except S,
which has none). The recursion with the table reproduces the census at n = 6 and n = 7
exactly, which is an independent consistency check between `check21` and `check23`.

At dimension 32 the same split recurs. `check23` §3 applies Bales's eight true products
to each 16-dimensional class and finds only its standard and its mirror double:
CD(·) = T for all four (absorption), and M(S), M(S′), **M(X16.2) = X32.6**,
**M(X16.3) = X32.7 = M³(H)**. So X32.6 and X32.7 are "uniform" (mirrors of a degenerate
class), while **X32.3, X32.4, X32.5** — the relative mirrors of T with respect to its three
orbits of *non-founding* copies of S — are, like X16.2, not any doubling product applied to
any of their hyperplanes. They share the hyperplane census (2 S, 1 S′, 21 X16.2, 7 X16.3) and
are separated by zero-divisor count (748, 676, 772) and by Der (6, **14**, 6): X32.4, the
mirror of T with respect to CD(O) ⊂ S ⊕ Sℓ′, keeps a full g₂.

Two things to read off. **The mirror of a mirror is the standard double**: R_{P4}(H) = O and
R_{S′}(O) = S on the founding copies — mirroring is an involution on the founding hyperplane,
which is why the paper's S′ ⊂ T ⊃ S picture is symmetric. And **the 32-dimensional classes are
in bijection with the Aut-orbits of hyperplanes of T** (multiplicities 1, 1, 1, 7, 7, 7, 7),
which is Theorem 3 in its sharp form at k = 4: the bound |L_4| ≤ 1 + 7 is attained.

## What is actually degenerate about X16.2 and X16.3

| | S | S′ | X16.2 | X16.3 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| in {CD, M} words | CD(O) | M(O) | **none** | M(M(H)) = M²(H) |
| as a relative mirror | — | R_S(O) | R_S(O_L), L a Fano line | R_S(P4) |
| octonion / quasi-octonion hyperplanes | 8 / 7 | 8 / 7 | **2 / 13** | **0 / 15** |
| two-term zero divisors (index pairs) | 84 (42) | 112 (56) | **148 (74)** | **160 (80)** |
| dim Ann of those | {4: 42} | {2: 42, 6: 14} | {2: 24, 4: 18, 6: 32} | {2: 18, 4: 24, 6: 38} |
| Der | g₂ (14) | g₂ (14) | **so(4) (6)** | **so(4) (6)** |
| graded automorphisms / signs | 168 = GL(3,2) | 168 | **24** | **24** |
| Bales double of one of its hyperplanes? | yes (P₃ᵀ on O) | yes (P₁ᵀ on O) | **no, by any of the 32** | yes (P₁ᵀ on P4) |
| quaternion property, flexibility, xx̄ = \|x\|², unit | yes | yes | yes | yes |

The last row is the point. **The degenerate subalgebras violate none of the formal
Cayley–Dickson-like axioms** — they are unital, quadratic, flexible, carry the involution and
the norm identity, every pair of basis elements generates a quaternion algebra, and their
twists are proper in Bales's sense. What they lose is *symmetry*: the derivation algebra drops
from g₂ to so(4) ≅ su(2) ⊕ su(2), the graded automorphism count from 168 to 24, and with the
symmetry go the octonion hyperplanes (8 → 2 → 0) and the sharp zero-divisor geometry. They
have exactly the symmetry of the quasi-octonions P4 = M(H) (Der = so(4), 24 graded
automorphisms), of which they are mostly made.

**X16.3 = M(M(H)) is a tower node** of the {CD, M} tree — the fourth algebra at dimension 16
that Remark 3.8 of the paper already names ("no octonion subalgebra among its basis
hyperplanes and Der of dimension 6"). By Corollary 4 of the relative-erasure proof it *had* to
be a hyperplane of T. So the [0+15] hyperplane of T that Cawagas et al. recorded is not a new
object: it is the double mirror of the quaternions.

**X16.2 is the genuinely new one.** It is not CD or M of anything, and it is not any of
Bales's 32 doubling products applied to either of its hyperplane types (O gives only S, S′;
P4 gives only S, X16.3). It is the mirror of S taken with respect to one of the seven octaves
through ℓ instead of the founding octave — and S is *not* the standard double of those
octaves (if it were, Theorem 3.3 would make X16.2 ≅ S′). This is the first basis subalgebra of
the tower that cannot be reached by any doubling formula from any of its hyperplanes. Its two
octaves O_L and R-partner sit in the ambient S as a Fano line L ⊂ PG(2,2) together with ℓ.

### The zero-divisor rule (`check24`)

Wilmot's mechanism — 12 two-term zero-divisor pairs internal to each quasi-octonion
hyperplane — holds in all four classes. The union is governed by one uniform rule:

> **dim Ann(e_i ± e_j) = 2 × (number of quasi-octonion hyperplanes in which e_i ± e_j is a
> zero divisor).**

| | P4 hyperplanes | incidence 12 × #P4 | multiplicity profile | pairs |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| S | 7 | 84 | {2: 42} | 42 |
| S′ | 7 | 84 | {1: 42, 3: 14} | 56 |
| X16.2 | 13 | 156 | {1: 24, 2: 18, 3: 32} | 74 |
| X16.3 | 15 | 180 | {1: 18, 2: 24, 3: 38} | 80 |

and in every row the (dim Ann, multiplicity) pairs are exactly (2,1), (4,2), (6,3). The
annihilator of a two-term zero divisor is the direct sum of its 2-dimensional annihilators
inside the quasi-octonion hyperplanes that contain it. This extends the Wilmot incidence
result of `mirror-tower.md` from S, S′ to the whole 16-dimensional landscape and explains the
annihilator profiles in the table above with no further input.

## Bales's rejected products never appear (`check23` §3)

All 24 of Bales's non-Cayley–Dickson products fail the quaternion property when applied to
H, O, P4, S, S′, X16.2 and X16.3 alike; the 8 true products always give exactly the standard
and the mirror double of the base (4 + 4). Since the quaternion property is a statement about
triples of basis elements, it is inherited by every basis subalgebra, and every basis
subalgebra of a Cayley–Dickson algebra has it (all 155 / 651 four-dimensional basis
subalgebras of T / A_6 are H). Hence:

> **No algebra built from a Bales-rejected product is ever a basis subalgebra of a
> Cayley–Dickson algebra, and the degenerate subalgebras are not Bales-degenerate.** Their
> degeneracy is invisible to the twisted-group-algebra axioms and lives entirely in the
> associator pattern.

## Wilmot's P4, P12, P14 (`check22`)

Wilmot presents the power-associative 8-dimensional subalgebras of A_n as a series P_k with
P4 in S, P12 first in A_5, P14 first in A_6, "and only these three P_k are embedded in A_n,
n ≥ 6". The census finds **only two** 8-dimensional classes at every level, O and P4 = M(H),
with counts 8/7, 50/105, 310/1085 that satisfy his Theorem 8 (S_{m+1} = 7(O_m + S_m):
105 = 7·15, 1085 = 7·155) exactly. His stated generating triads (o₁, o₂₄, o₃₄₅) ⊂ A_5 for
P12 and (o₁₄, o₂₅, o₃₆) ⊂ A_6 for P14 both span subalgebras that are **graded-isomorphic to
P4** (explicit σ found). So P12 and P14 are isomorphic to P4 as algebras; the distinction is
one of embedding ("up to representation", as his abstract says), not of isomorphism type.
This is consistent with Theorem 3: the 8-dimensional landscape is complete at A_4.

## The associator pattern (the tool)

For anticommutative sign-monomial algebras, define assoc(p,q,r) = w(p,q)w(p⊕q,r)w(q,r)w(p,q⊕r)
∈ {±1}, i.e. the sign with which (e_p e_q)e_r and e_p(e_q e_r) differ.

> Two such algebras are graded-isomorphic iff some σ ∈ GL(n,2) carries one associator
> pattern onto the other.

(⇐: with the commutator and diagonal signs matched, the ratio of the twists is a symmetric
2-cocycle with trivial diagonal, whose extension is elementary abelian and therefore splits —
so the ratio is a coboundary, i.e. a choice of signs λ. ⇒ is the usual coboundary
computation.) This replaces the paper's enumeration of GL(4,2) × sign solutions with a pruned
backtracking search on σ, which agrees with `f2iso.py` on every test (168, 168, 0
automorphism counts and 60/60 random Bales pairs) and runs at dimension 32 and 64. It also
says what a class *is*: a GL(n,2)-orbit of associator patterns. Wilmot's "non-associative
triads" are the support of this pattern; his silo counts are pattern statistics.

## What would change my mind

- **A 2^k-dimensional basis subalgebra outside L_k(k+1) at some n ≥ k+2.** Theorem 3 says
  there is none; the search through A_7 at dimension 16 (11 811 subgroups) found none. A
  counterexample would mean a hole in the induction of Corollary 2, most likely in the
  identification of the graph subgroups with relative mirrors.
- **A hyperplane of A_{k+1} through the top unit not isomorphic to A_k.** Would break
  absorption; checked exhaustively for k ≤ 5.
- **A basis subalgebra failing the quaternion property.** Would reopen the door for Bales's
  rejected products. Checked for all 4-dimensional subgroups through A_6 (651 of them).

## What this is worth

For the paper: Remark 3.8 and claim (A) become instances of one statement, and the [2+13]
and [0+15] hyperplanes that Cawagas recorded acquire names and constructions. The sharper
message for v2 is that **S′ is the first of a family generated by relative mirroring, and CD
erases all of it in one step.** The relative-erasure theorem is two lines longer than the
erasure theorem and strictly more general; it should replace it.

For the tower sequel: the landscape L_k is the natural object, its generation by
R_{A_k}(·) and its stability are theorems, and the class count **1, 2, 4, 8, 16** at
dimensions 4 … 64 is the number to explain. The conjecture on the table is
**|L_k| = 2^{k−2}**, equivalently that the graded automorphism group of A_k has exactly
2^{k−2} − 1 orbits on the hyperplanes of A_k, each with a distinct relative mirror. The orbit
data so far: 1 (O); 1 + 1 + 1 (S: founding, 7 through ℓ, 7 quasi — but the two 7-orbits give
X16.2 and X16.3, so 3 orbits); 7 orbits of sizes 1,1,1,7,7,7,7 (T); 15 orbits of sizes
1⁷, 7⁸ (A_6). Hyperplanes of A_k are the points of PG(k−1, 2) (2^k − 1 of them), so the
conjecture says the graded automorphisms of A_k act on that projective space with
2^{k−2} − 1 orbits — a statement about a finite group action that should be provable
from the recursive structure of the twist.
